\begin{abstract}
AI weather models such as ECMWF's AIFS-Single forecast tropical cyclone tracks well but
systematically underestimate intensity, because a $0.25^{\circ}$ grid cannot resolve the eyewall.
\citet{allen2026aifstc} showed that a simple machine-learned correction, trained on tabular features
of the forecast itself, removes most of this bias. This report reproduces the gradient-boosted half
of that work at reduced scale, using only public data: 399 operational AIFS-Single forecasts
covering every North Atlantic and East Pacific storm from February 2025 to September 2026, tracked
with a purpose-built MSLP-minimum tracker and corrected with gradient-boosted trees under
storm-grouped cross-validation. The raw model's maximum-wind mean absolute error (MAE) of
30.3\,kt is more than halved to 14.4\,kt, against 9.4\,kt for the official NHC forecast.
Raw-model error and the rapid-intensification failure mode match the paper almost exactly
(71.4\,kt vs.\ their 71.1\,kt raw MAE on RI cases), while the corrected error remains above the
paper's 11.0\,kt, consistent with training on one and a half seasons instead of nine years.
The tracker is validated against ECMWF's own operational AIFS tracks to within 1.1\,kt MAE.
The second half of the report asks whether a learned correction can be made to respect the
observed tail of the intensity distribution, and what that buys. Eta-learning
\citep{chang2026eta}, a tail-matching penalty on a small neural network, beats a tuned MSE network
by 1.1\,kt overall (95\% storm-bootstrap CI $[-2.3, -0.03]$) and beats the classical post-hoc
quantile-mapping baseline by 2.1\,kt; most of its advantage over an untuned network, however, is
regularization. Scored by proper scores rather than by error on cases selected for having
intensified, no point model separates from the GBM: by a tail-weighted CRPS above 96\,kt eta is
$+0.3$\,kt $[-0.6, +1.3]$ from the GBM, although it has the best hit rate at 96\,kt (0.54 against
0.28, at the same false-alarm ratio). A diagnosis of \emph{which} storms receive the extreme
forecasts shows the penalty controls how many extreme forecasts are issued, not where they go.
The constructive response is a probabilistic correction: quantile gradient boosting with a
cross-conformal recalibration step cuts the CRPS of the point GBM on the same features by a
third (9.4 against 13.6\,kt in cross-validation, 10.7 against 14.0 on a future season), improves the tail-weighted
CRPS by 0.8\,kt $[-1.4, -0.2]$, and gives 80\% intervals that cover 82\% of out-of-storm cases
and 82\% of the future season. SHIPS-type environmental predictors computed from the AIFS
pressure-level fields add little: 0.2\,kt overall and 0.8\,kt at short leads, with intervals
that include zero. A quantile model of the 24\,h intensity change itself
gives the first probability of RI with skill over climatology in cross-validation (Brier skill
$+0.09$, AUC 0.80), where reading the probability off the intensity distribution or a direct
classifier has none; on the future season all three fall below climatology. The whole record
holds 15 RI storms.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Data-driven weather models have reached operational skill for synoptic-scale prediction, and
ECMWF's AIFS-Single \citep{lang2024aifs} runs operationally alongside the physics-based IFS. For
tropical cyclones these models inherit a structural weakness: with grid spacing of about 28\,km,
the inner core of a hurricane is unresolved, so forecast 10\,m winds saturate far below observed
intensity even when the track is accurate. \citet{allen2026aifstc} (``AIFS-TC'') demonstrated that
this bias is largely \emph{predictable}: a gradient-boosted model over simple features of the AIFS
forecast and the operationally known initial intensity brings AIFS intensity forecasts to parity
with the operational frontier.

This project asks how much of that result survives a drastic reduction in resources: public data
only, one and a half hurricane seasons instead of nine years of hindcasts, a home-made tracker, and
a laptop. Beyond the replication itself, the by-product is an end-to-end open pipeline (catalog,
fetch, track, correct, evaluate, environmental predictors, ensemble members).

The reproduction exposes the weakness that motivates the rest of the report: the corrected
forecasts never reach the observed extremes. Trees cannot extrapolate beyond their training
targets, so the GBM never forecasts above about 125\,kt while nine storms in the sample reached
120\,kt or more. Sections~\ref{sec:eta} to \ref{sec:prob} study three responses in turn: a
tail-matching training objective (eta-learning), richer inputs (environmental predictors of the
kind used by the operational SHIPS scheme), and a probabilistic output with calibrated intervals.
Every comparison is reported as a paired difference with a storm-level bootstrap interval, because
the effective sample is the number of storms (52), not the number of cases (9149).

\section{Data}

\paragraph{AIFS forecasts.} The ECMWF Open Data AWS mirror (\texttt{s3://ecmwf-forecasts},
CC-BY-4.0) retains every operational AIFS-Single run since 2025-02-25. Storm lifetimes from the
IBTrACS best-track archive \citep{knapp2010ibtracs} define 399 forecast initializations (00/12
UTC) covering all North Atlantic (NA) and East Pacific (EP) storms of at least tropical-storm
strength through 2026-09-08. For each run, only the GRIB messages for mean sea-level pressure and
10\,m winds are downloaded via HTTP byte ranges, cropped to $0$--$60^{\circ}$N,
$180^{\circ}$W--$0^{\circ}$, at leads 0--168\,h every 6\,h (7.1\,GB total, versus hundreds of GB
for the full archive).

\paragraph{Reduction caveats.} The stored arrays are a lossy reduction: three surface variables,
one basin pair, leads capped at 168\,h (the mirror holds 360\,h), and winds stored in half
precision (error well below $0.1$\,kt, negligible against forecast error).

\paragraph{Pressure-level fields for environmental predictors.} For Section~\ref{sec:ships}, ten
further fields were pulled for every run and lead from the same archive (via its Google Cloud
mirror, which does not rate-limit byte-range requests): $u$ and $v$ at 850 and 200\,hPa,
temperature at 200, 500 and 700\,hPa, specific humidity at 500 and 700\,hPa, and skin
temperature. Cropped to the same box this adds 23\,GB. The tabular dataset used from
Section~\ref{sec:eta} onward is the full set of 9149 labelled (storm, initialization, lead)
cases from 52 storms, which is larger than the homogeneous sample of Section~\ref{sec:results}
because it does not require an OFCL forecast to exist at the same lead.

\paragraph{Operational archive, not hindcasts.} Unlike the paper's controlled hindcast training
set, these are the forecasts actually issued in real time. Two consequences matter. First, the
model version changes mid-dataset: AIFS-Single v1 was operational from 25 February 2025, v1.1
from 27 August 2025 (after a one-day trial on 31 July), and v2 from 12 May 2026. A flag marking
runs from 12 May 2026 is passed to the correction model, so the trees can separate v2 from the
earlier versions; v1 and v1.1 are not distinguished. Results are reported by season. Second, each run starts from the real-time operational analysis with its
data-assimilation cutoff, not from a reanalysis.

\paragraph{Verification and benchmarks.} Ground truth is the IBTrACS/ATCF best track (USA agency
values). The benchmark is OFCL, the official human forecast of the National Hurricane Center,
parsed from the ATCF a-deck archive, which also supplies GDMN, the ensemble mean of Google
DeepMind's cyclone model \citep{deepmind2025weatherlab}, and EAIO, ECMWF's own tracker applied to
the same AIFS runs.

\section{Methods}

\paragraph{Tracker.} Each forecast is seeded at the best-track position at initialization. At each
6\,h step the storm is located at the MSLP minimum within 450\,km of a first guess (previous
position advected with previous motion) on a lightly smoothed field; intensity is that minimum
pressure and the maximum 10\,m wind within 250\,km. Tracking stops when the low is no longer
distinct. Against ECMWF's operational AIFS tracks (EAIO) at matched initialization and lead
($n=1209$, 24 storms), the tracker agrees to a mean difference of $+0.8$\,kt and an MAE of
1.1\,kt, so tracker choice is not a meaningful error source.

\paragraph{Correction model.} Following the GBM branch of AIFS-TC, gradient-boosted trees
(scikit-learn \texttt{HistGradientBoostingRegressor}, 5-seed average) predict the residual between
best-track and AIFS maximum wind. The 22 features include the AIFS forecast state and its trends
and running extremes, translation speed, basin, day of year, the model-version flag, storm age,
the observed intensity and 12/24\,h intensity trend at initialization, and the initial AIFS bias.
Observed rapid-intensification cases (RI, $\ge 30$\,kt observed gain in 24\,h) are weighted
$2\times$ in training, as in the paper. Evaluation uses storm-grouped 5-fold cross-validation, so
a storm is never corrected by a model that saw any part of it. A persistence-of-initial-bias
baseline (carrying the initialization-time AIFS bias forward unchanged) is included as a sanity
check; the GBM beats it at every lead beyond 24\,h.

\section{Evaluation protocol for everything that follows}\label{sec:protocol}

Four conventions hold for Sections~\ref{sec:eta} to \ref{sec:prob}. (i) Models predict the
residual $r = v_{\mathrm{obs}} - v_{\mathrm{AIFS}}$ and are scored on the corrected wind
$v_{\mathrm{AIFS}} + \hat r$. (ii) Cross-validation is storm-grouped and five-fold; a temporal
holdout (train on 2025 initializations, test on 2026: 20 storms, 4121 cases, 222 RI cases from
4 storms) is reported where the model is cheap enough to refit. (iii) Any difference between two
models is a \emph{paired} difference, with a 95\% interval from resampling storms with
replacement 2000 times. Treating cases as independent would understate the uncertainty by a
factor of two or more. A check on twenty years of NHC forecasts from the ATCF archive (573
storms, two pairs of operational models; draw 50 storms at random, build a nominal 95\% interval
for the paired MAE difference by each method, see whether it covers the full-archive value,
repeat) found that intervals built on cases cover 65 to 73\% of the time, while the storm
bootstrap covers 95\% overall but only 86 to 93\% on extreme subsets. Every number on an extreme subset therefore
also states the number of storms behind it. (iv) Subsets defined by the observed outcome (RI
cases, cases observed at 96\,kt or more) select on the thing being forecast, so error on them
rewards any model that forecasts high; they are reported as diagnostics of where the error lives,
not as scores. The scores that rank models are the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS;
for a point forecast it reduces to the absolute error, so point and distributional models share
one scale; for the quantile model it is the exact CRPS of the piecewise-linear distribution
through its nine quantiles, since the common shortcut of twice the mean pinball loss over the
levels understates it by about a third when the levels crowd the tails), a threshold-weighted CRPS \citep{gneiting2011twcrps} that scores only the part of the
distribution above 96\,kt (the CRPS of forecast and observation both censored at 96\,kt, so a
forecast and an observation that both stay below the threshold score zero), and the hit rate (POD)
and false-alarm ratio (FAR) of forecasting at least 96 and 120\,kt. Table~\ref{tab:headline}
gives these for every model in the report; the sections that follow explain each model. Read
this way the point models are closer than their RI columns suggest: by the
tail-weighted CRPS no point model separates from the GBM, eta included, while eta detects far more
of the 96\,kt cases at the same false-alarm ratio. The quantile model is the only one that improves
both scores (against the point GBM on its own features: $-4.2$ and $-0.8$\,kt, both intervals
excluding zero).

\section{Eta-learning: a tail-matching objective}\label{sec:eta}

\paragraph{Method.} \citet{chang2026eta} propose training a regressor with the usual mean-squared
error plus a penalty that pulls the upper quantiles of its \emph{predictions} toward the same
quantiles of a reference distribution $\nu_0$ of the target. Here the network is a 64-64 ReLU
multilayer perceptron on the 22 features of Section~3, trained full-batch with Adam
(learning rate $3\times10^{-3}$, 600 iterations, three seeds averaged), the penalty is the mean
absolute gap between 25 quantiles (levels 0.95 to 0.999) of the corrected wind over the training
fold and the same quantiles of the observed wind in that fold, and the weight $\lambda$ is set
by the paper's balancing rule, the ratio of the gradient norms of the two terms, re-evaluated
every 100 iterations (the paper applies the rule once or adaptively; the schedule is this
report's choice). The
plain-MSE network with the penalty switched off (``ERM'') is the natural control.

\paragraph{Ablation.} Table~\ref{tab:eta} separates the three things that differ between the
paper's GBM and the eta network: architecture, objective, and the $2\times$ RI weighting.

The clean contrast is eta against ERM on the same network: $-4.6$\,kt $[-6.8, -2.2]$. Against
the GBM the overall difference is a tie ($-0.7$\,kt $[-2.0, +0.8]$), but on RI cases eta wins by
$-10.5$\,kt $[-14.4, -5.6]$. Eta is also the only model whose forecasts reach the observed tail:
its 0.95 and 0.99 forecast quantiles are 119 and 143\,kt against 120 and 140 observed, whereas the
GBM's are 98 and 118. Table~\ref{tab:eta} and the differences in this paragraph are from the
first run of the eta notebook, against the unweighted GBM; Tables~\ref{tab:fair} and
\ref{tab:fairdiff} are from the later fair-baseline run, which retrained every model with shared
folds and seeds against the $2\times$ RI-weighted GBM. The two runs agree to 0.3\,kt overall and
1\,kt on RI. On proper scores (Table~\ref{tab:headline}) the RI margin disappears: eta
and the GBM differ by $+0.37$\,kt $[-0.54, +1.34]$ of tail-weighted CRPS, a tie. What eta buys is
detection: it forecasts 96\,kt or more for 54\% of the cases that reached it, against 28\% for the
GBM, at the same false-alarm ratio (0.29).

\paragraph{Is it calibration or climatology?} The reference $\nu_0$ above is the 18-month
training sample itself, so the penalty can only be \emph{calibrating} the network to a
distribution it already sees. The version that imports information uses $\nu_0$ from the
IBTrACS archive since 1980 (1651 storms). The result barely moves: MAE 12.8 against 13.1,
difference $-0.3$\,kt $[-1.3, +0.8]$, and on RI $+2.3$\,kt $[-1.4, +5.9]$. Eta is doing
calibration, not importing climatology.

\paragraph{Lead time and the future season.} Eta's advantage over ERM grows with lead
($-3.7$, $-4.0$, $-4.7$, $-5.3$, $-5.8$\,kt across the bands 6--24, 30--48, 54--72, 78--120 and
126--168\,h, every interval excluding zero), contrary to the expectation that matching a point
forecast to the observed tail should hurt at long leads, where the best point forecast is less
extreme than the observations. The reason is visible in the tail itself: at 126--168\,h eta's
0.99 forecast quantile is 134\,kt against 153 observed, so the penalty is no longer binding
there. On the temporal holdout (train 2025, test 2026), which also tests transfer across model
versions since the 2025 runs are AIFS-Single v1 and v1.1 and most 2026 runs are v2, the picture is
unchanged: raw 27.8\,kt, GBM 15.8 (base features, $2\times$ RI weighting, as in Table~\ref{tab:eta}),
ERM 22.4, eta 15.6; eta minus GBM $-0.2$\,kt $[-3.0, +3.5]$ overall and $-7.9$\,kt
$[-15.3, -0.5]$ on RI, the latter resting on four storms.

\subsection{The fair baseline: a tuned network plus quantile mapping}\label{sec:fair}

The ERM network above has no early stopping, and its tail overshoots (0.99 quantile 160\,kt
against 140 observed), so part of eta's gain could be regularization rather than the objective.
The fair comparison is (a) the same network with early stopping and
weight decay chosen on held-out training storms, and (b) that network followed by quantile
mapping of its corrected winds onto $\nu_0$, the classical marginal correction of climate science
\citep{maraun2013inflation}. If eta still wins against (b), it is doing more than matching a
histogram. All five models in Table~\ref{tab:fair} were trained in one run with identical folds
and seeds.

\subsection{Right tails, wrong storms}\label{sec:wrongstorms}

Matching the pooled tail does not mean the extreme forecasts land on the right storms. Among
eta's 440 forecasts of 120\,kt or more, the observed wind is on average 22\,kt lower
($[-33.5, +1.7]$), and 358 of the 440 come from two storms (Lowell and Erin). Of the 636 cases
observed at 120\,kt or more, eta forecasts 120\,kt or more for 26\%. This is not specific to eta:
the untuned network overshoots by 34\,kt on its own extreme forecasts, and the GBM followed by
quantile mapping shows the same pattern ($-23$\,kt). It is the generic behaviour of a point
forecast whose marginal has been forced onto the observed one, and the eta paper states the
limitation itself (``distributionally calibrated candidate extremes, rather than \ldots\ true
location, time, or mechanism''). An attempt to fix it by applying the penalty within groups
(by initial intensity) was abandoned once the group-wise gaps were bootstrapped: every interval
includes zero. The constructive response is to stop forcing a point forecast and predict a
distribution instead (Section~\ref{sec:prob}).

\section{Environmental predictors from the forecast itself}\label{sec:ships}

The 22 features so far describe the AIFS vortex (wind, pressure, their trends) and the storm's
history, nothing about its environment. The operational SHIPS scheme
\citep{demaria1994ships, demaria2005ships} and its RI index \citep{kaplan2003ri} rest on a handful
of environmental quantities: vertical wind shear, mid-level humidity, upper-level divergence and
temperature, low-level vorticity, and the ocean's potential intensity. All of them can be
computed from the AIFS forecast's own pressure-level fields, which makes them legitimate
forecast-time features. From the fields of Section~2, each case receives: the magnitude and
direction of the ring-mean (200 to 800\,km from the forecast centre) 200 minus 850\,hPa wind
vector; relative humidity at 700 and 500\,hPa from $q$ and $T$; 200\,hPa divergence and 850\,hPa
relative vorticity within 1000\,km; 200\,hPa temperature; skin temperature within 200\,km as a
sea-surface proxy; the \citet{demaria1994mpi} maximum potential intensity from it; and the
potential minus the current forecast wind. Each is taken at the forecast lead, at
initialization, and averaged along the forecast track, for 36 features in all.

Table~\ref{tab:ships} gives the effect on the point GBM of Section~\ref{sec:prob}'s code, the
same fits as the GBM rows of Tables~\ref{tab:headline} and \ref{tab:prob}. The gain is small:
$-0.2$\,kt overall and $-0.8$ at short leads, both intervals including zero, nothing on RI, and a
loss at long leads. An earlier version of this table from a separate GBM fit showed $-0.9$\,kt
overall and $-1.1$ at short leads, so the short-lead gain is probably real but its size depends
on the fit. The advisor's hypothesis was that
the information bottleneck, not the objective, limits RI skill; these predictors do not lift it,
which leaves the AIFS environment at $0.25^{\circ}$, the single deterministic run, or the sample
of 15 RI storms as the remaining suspects.

\section{A probabilistic correction}\label{sec:prob}

\paragraph{Method.} Nine gradient-boosted regressors with the pinball loss predict the residual
quantiles at levels 0.02, 0.05, 0.10, 0.25, 0.50, 0.75, 0.90, 0.95 and 0.98 (same features as
Table~\ref{tab:ships} with SHIPS, same tree hyperparameters as the point GBM, quantiles sorted to
remove crossings). Unlike the point GBM, the quantile models carry no $2\times$ weight on RI
cases: a distributional model should not have its loss reweighted by the outcome, and dropping the
weights also scores slightly better (CRPS 9.41 against 9.55 in CV, 10.71 against 10.87 on the
holdout). Quantile trees turned out to be far too confident on
unseen storms: the raw 10--90\% interval covered only 51\% of out-of-fold cases. The remedy is a
cross-conformal recalibration in the spirit of \citet{romano2019cqr} and \citet{barber2021cvplus}:
within each training fold, an inner storm-grouped 5-fold produces out-of-fold residuals
$y - \hat q_\tau(x)$ for every level $\tau$; the empirical $\tau$-quantile of those residuals,
computed separately in three lead bands (0--48, 54--96, 102--168\,h), is added to $\hat q_\tau$
at test time. The offset is a constant in knots within each band. The alternative, an offset
proportional to the predicted 10--90\% width so that the cases the trees
already mark as uncertain are widened most, was also run: it lifts the 80\% coverage of RI cases
in CV from 32 to 54\% and of cases observed at 96\,kt or more from 52 to 66\%, but widens every
ordinary case with them and loses on every proper score (CRPS 9.52 against 9.41 in CV, 11.63
against 10.71 on the holdout, where its intervals cover 91\%), so the constant version is kept.
Scores are the mean absolute error of the median, the CRPS \citep{gneiting2007scoring} of the
piecewise-linear distribution through the nine quantiles (Section~\ref{sec:protocol}), the
threshold-weighted CRPS, and the coverage of the 10--90\% interval. The probability of RI is treated separately below.

\paragraph{Calibration.} Figure~\ref{fig:calib} shows the fraction of observations falling
below each predicted quantile. The raw quantile trees are badly over-confident (10--90\% interval
covering 51\%); after recalibration the curve sits on the diagonal in cross-validation (82\%) and
the CRPS improves from 10.12 to 9.41\,kt. On the future season the 10--90\% interval still covers
82\%, but the curve sits above the diagonal at every level: the 2026 observations fall below the
predicted median 69\% of the time, because the offsets were learned on the 2025 storms, which
include the two strongest of the record, and push every quantile up. That shift costs CRPS there
(10.71 against 10.29 for the unrecalibrated quantiles, whose intervals cover 51\%): on the
holdout the recalibration buys coverage and pays for it in sharpness. Calibration is not uniform
across cases either: on RI cases the 80\% interval covers only 32\% in CV, and on cases observed
at 96\,kt or more it covers 52\%. The price of covering ordinary cases honestly while being unable
to tell which storms will intensify is a wide upper tail: the 0.99 quantile of the predicted 95th
percentile is 167\,kt in CV against 140 observed. The model knows it does not know. The pooled
tail of \emph{samples} drawn from the predictive distribution, which is where a tail
constraint would belong if one were needed, matches the observed tail in cross-validation without
any penalty: 103, 118 and 143\,kt at levels 0.9, 0.95 and 0.99 against 110, 120 and 140 observed.
The per-level recalibration achieves what the eta penalty achieves for a point forecast, and does
it on a distribution, where it is coherent. On the 2026 holdout the sampled tail is too heavy (132
and 157\,kt at 0.95 and 0.99 against 120 and 135), for the same reason as the shifted curve.

\paragraph{Accuracy.} Table~\ref{tab:prob} compares the median of the recalibrated distribution
with the point GBM trained on the same features. In cross-validation the median is better by
$0.96$\,kt $[-1.89, -0.09]$ overall and by $1.7$\,kt $[-3.3, -0.0]$ at leads beyond 96\,h; on RI
cases and on cases observed at 96\,kt or more the differences ($-1.2$ and $-1.8$\,kt) have
intervals that include zero. On the 2026 holdout the median is $0.3$\,kt better overall
$[-1.45, +0.82]$, no worse at short leads, and $5.6$\,kt $[-7.6, -3.3]$ better on the cases
observed at 96\,kt or more, where the point GBM's trees are capped by their training targets. On
the proper scores the distribution beats the point GBM on the same features by 4.2\,kt
$[-5.1, -3.2]$ of CRPS in CV and 3.3 $[-4.8, -1.9]$ on the holdout, and by $0.8$\,kt
$[-1.4, -0.2]$ of tail-weighted CRPS in CV ($-0.9$ $[-2.3, +0.5]$ on the holdout); its median
also forecasts 96\,kt for 58\% of the holdout
cases that reached it, against 39\% for the point GBM, at the same false-alarm ratio. The
probabilistic model costs nothing in point accuracy and adds calibrated intervals.

\paragraph{Probability of rapid intensification.} This is where the project set out to arrive.
Reading $P(\mathrm{RI})$ off the predicted intensity distribution, as
$P(v_{t} \ge v_{t-24} + 30\,\mathrm{kt})$ with $v_{t-24}$ the predicted median at the earlier
lead (the observed intensity when $t = 24$\,h), has no skill: Brier skill score $-0.03$ against
climatology in cross-validation and $-0.38$ on the holdout, and the reliability diagram
(Figure~\ref{fig:rel}) shows why: when it says 0.4 to 0.6, RI follows 21 to 23\% of the time in
CV and 3 to 11\% on the holdout. The construction is also wrong in principle: the marginals at two leads are predicted separately, and their difference is not the
distribution of the 24\,h change. The fix is to model the change directly. The same nine quantile
regressors with the same cross-conformal step, trained on the observed change $v_t - v_{t-24}$
over the 7317 cases at leads of 24\,h or more (477 RI cases, 51 storms), give
$P(\mathrm{RI}) = 1 - \hat F(30)$. This is the first version with skill: Brier skill $+0.09$ in
cross-validation, area under the ROC curve 0.80, and a reliability curve on the diagonal up to
0.2 that then \emph{under}-forecasts (when it says 0.35, RI follows 78\% of the time, over 36
cases). Its median is also a better forecast of the change than the raw AIFS change (MAE 11.7
against 12.5\,kt) and than persistence (14.8). Paired by storm, it beats the intensity route by
0.01 $[0.00, 0.02]$ in Brier score in CV and by 0.02 $[0.01, 0.03]$ on the holdout. On the
holdout, however, its own skill is negative again ($-0.09$, AUC 0.75): it issues probabilities
between 0.3 and 0.5 for 171 cases of 2026, of which 4\% intensified, the same upward shift that
moves the intensity quantiles. The direct classifier on the RI label is $-0.02$ in CV and
$-0.73$ on the holdout (AUC 0.68 and 0.69). With discrimination of 0.75 to 0.80 for the two quantile routes and
calibration that holds within the training distribution but not across seasons, the reading is
that 15 RI storms are enough to rank cases and not enough to attach a probability that transfers.
The warning trade-off is steep either way: at $p \ge 0.2$ the change model catches 17\% of RI
cases with 55\% false alarms in CV (17\% and 92\% on the holdout). The eta network's RI advantage
of Section~\ref{sec:fair} is a gain in the \emph{magnitude} of its forecasts on storms that did
intensify, not an ability to say in advance which storms will.

\paragraph{Ensemble members as a probability.} The other route is to
correct each of the 51 AIFS-ENS members and use the fraction that intensify by 30\,kt as
$P(\mathrm{RI})$. On the 2462 cases of the ensemble subset (Section~\ref{sec:dnw}) that have a member value
24\,h earlier (31 storms, 202 RI cases), no raw member ever intensifies by 30\,kt in 24\,h, so the
fraction is identically zero (AUC 0.50): the members are as unresolved as the deterministic run.
After each member is passed through the out-of-fold point GBM with its own wind, pressure and
24\,h change substituted for the deterministic ones, the fraction reaches AUC 0.67 and Brier
skill $-0.02$. The direct change model on the same cases has $+0.09$ and AUC 0.82, better by
0.01 $[0.00, 0.01]$ in Brier score. The corrected members are one correction applied to 51
nearly identical inputs, not 51 intensification scenarios.

\paragraph{Operational initial intensity.} Everything above uses the best-track intensity at
initialisation as a feature, where a real-time system only has the CARQ estimate from the ATCF
a-deck, so the comparison with the official forecast is tilted in the model's favour. Swapping
CARQ in for the best track at initialisation (available for 7528 of 9149 cases, 48 storms; the
two differ by 1.1\,kt on average, since the 2025 and 2026 best tracks are still close to the
operational estimates) changes nothing in cross-validation (CRPS 9.45 against 9.41, median MAE
12.69 against 12.68, change-model Brier skill $+0.09$ either way) and costs a little on the
holdout: median MAE 14.49 against 13.68, point GBM 14.86 against 13.96, coverage 76\% against
82\%, CRPS 11.08 against 10.71. The best-track numbers are kept as the main ones so that the earlier
sections stay comparable; the operational penalty on this data is under 1\,kt.

\section{What did not work}\label{sec:dnw}

Three further ideas were tested and dropped; all are recorded so that they are not retried.
\emph{Wind through pressure.} The observed wind-pressure relationship is tight (6.9\,kt MAE from
observed pressure), so correcting AIFS pressure and converting might beat correcting wind
directly. It does not: 15.2 against 14.0\,kt, worse by $+1.2$\,kt $[+0.1, +2.3]$, because the
correction removes 51\% of the wind error but only 13\% of the pressure error. \emph{An archive
prior.} A model of future intensity from storm history alone, trained on 45 years of IBTrACS with
no forecast input, reaches 17.6\,kt on these storms and beats the AIFS-based correction at 6--24\,h
(7.2 against 9.9). Stacked as a feature it helps when training storms are few ($-2.0$\,kt
$[-2.8, -1.1]$ with 10 storms) and not with all 41 ($-0.3$ $[-0.7, +0.2]$); a Bayesian
combination of the archive prior with an AIFS likelihood beat stacking only at 5 training storms
and was worse on RI. With a full season of paired data the archive adds nothing.
\emph{Ensemble spread.} From July 2025 the archive also holds the 51-member AIFS-ENS. For the 213
initialisations from 2 July 2025 that contain an RI storm, each member's maximum 10\,m wind and
minimum pressure within 250\,km of the deterministic track position were extracted at 6 to
120\,h (3323 cases, 33 storms, 263 RI cases) and summarised into eleven features: member mean,
spread, 10th and 90th percentiles, maximum, the control run, their differences from the
deterministic wind, the fraction of members above it, and the mean and spread of the member
minimum pressures. Trained and scored on this subset only, with and without the eleven features
added to base plus SHIPS, the point GBM changes by $-0.05$\,kt $[-0.63, +0.54]$, the quantile
median by $-0.38$ $[-0.90, +0.22]$, the CRPS by $-0.03$ $[-0.36, +0.42]$, and 80\% coverage
moves from 82 to 81\%; on RI cases the median moves by $-2.1$ $[-4.6, +0.2]$ and the point model
by $-1.7$ $[-4.1, +0.3]$. The spread does carry information: its Spearman correlation with the
absolute error of the raw forecast is 0.34, and with that of the point correction 0.29. But the
quantile model already has it from the deterministic features alone: the width of its 10 to
90\% interval correlates 0.59 with the member spread and 0.38 with its own median's error, a
similar figure. The member mean sits 0.8\,kt above the deterministic wind on average, yet on the strongest
storms every member is as weak as the deterministic run (the subset's observed maximum is
165\,kt; the deterministic run peaks at 66 and the strongest member at 79), so the
ensemble-minus-deterministic signals correlate negatively with the residual ($-0.34$, and $-0.44$
for the fraction of members above) and only restate the intensity the GBM already sees. The
spread would matter for a model that lacks the vortex features; stacked on top of them it adds
nothing measurable with 33 storms. Correcting each member and counting the fraction that
intensify, the other use of the ensemble, is reported in Section~\ref{sec:prob}.

\section{Limitations}

One and a half seasons versus nine years of training data, and 52 storms of which 15 produced RI
and 9 reached 120\,kt: every RI or tail statistic in this report rests on a handful of storms, and
the intervals say so. Three model versions in one dataset, handled only by a flag for the last. The GBM
branch of AIFS-TC only (no CNN over three-dimensional patches), and the ensemble spread tested
only on the RI-storm subset from July 2025, at leads to 120\,h.
Best-track initial intensity as a feature in the main numbers, with the CARQ variant reported
once (Section~\ref{sec:prob}); two basins; leads capped at 168\,h.
Track position is not corrected. The eta network was tuned once (the paper's
settings) and the quantile model inherits the point GBM's hyperparameters; neither was searched.
Model variants for the dropped ideas of Section~\ref{sec:wrongstorms} and the archive prior were
chosen after seeing results and are reported as negative findings only. The 24\,h change model,
the spread-scaled conformal variant and the member-fraction probability were added after the
advisor's comments, on the same 52 storms; the positive Brier skill of the change model in
cross-validation rests on 477 RI cases from 15 storms and does not transfer to 2026.
